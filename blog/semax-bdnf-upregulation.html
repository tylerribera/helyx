Semax and BDNF Upregulation: What 20 Years of Studies Have Shown

Semax has been studied since the 1980s as a modulator of BDNF and NGF expression. A review of the neurotrophic literature — from Ostrovskaya's rodent work to more recent human EEG studies — and what it means for research into cognitive-enhancing peptides.

By the Helyx research team · July 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Few nootropic peptides have as long a research paper trail as Semax. Developed at the Institute of Molecular Genetics of the Russian Academy of Sciences in the late 1980s as a heptapeptide analog of ACTH(4-10), Semax has been studied in stroke recovery, cognitive performance, and neurotrophic factor regulation for more than three decades. This is a short tour of what the literature actually says.

The molecule

Semax is a synthetic heptapeptide with the sequence Met-Glu-His-Phe-Pro-Gly-Pro. It is a truncated analog of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) fragments 4–10, engineered to preserve neurotropic activity while eliminating the corticotropic effects of the parent hormone.1 This structural choice matters: the neurotrophic signal is decoupled from HPA-axis activation, which is one reason the compound has been of interest to researchers studying isolated neuroplasticity pathways.

BDNF and NGF: the neurotrophic signal

The most-cited finding in the Semax literature is that acute administration in rodent models increases hippocampal expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and its receptor TrkB, along with nerve growth factor (NGF).2 Dolotov and colleagues reported measurable mRNA increases within hours, with protein-level increases following. This is the pathway that most subsequent nootropic-peptide research has anchored to.

Independent replications by Shadrina, Agapova, and colleagues extended these findings across multiple brain regions and showed that the neurotrophic response is dose-dependent and time-limited — not a persistent up-regulation, but a transient window during which downstream plasticity-related gene expression is elevated.3

The signal is real, transient, and dose-dependent. It is not a permanent rewiring — it is a window.

Human studies: EEG and cognitive performance

Human research on Semax is less extensive than the rodent literature, but not absent. Kaplan and colleagues published EEG studies in the 1990s and early 2000s showing changes in cognitive-evoked potentials following intranasal administration.4 More recent work has examined its use in ischemic stroke recovery, where a series of Russian clinical trials have reported improved neurological outcomes when Semax is administered in the acute post-stroke window.5

Two caveats matter for how to read this literature: most human trials have been conducted at Russian medical centers, and independent Western replication remains limited. The mechanistic case is strong; the translational human data are narrower than the neurochemistry alone might suggest.

The dopamine-serotonin adjacency

A separate line of research has examined Semax's downstream effects on monoaminergic systems. Studies have reported acute increases in dopamine and serotonin turnover in specific brain regions following administration, mediated in part by the neurotrophic pathway rather than by direct receptor binding.6 This is a distinguishing feature of Semax compared to classical dopaminergic or serotonergic compounds: the monoaminergic effect appears to be a downstream consequence of the neurotrophic signal, not an upstream cause.

What is still open

Three questions remain genuinely unresolved in the published record: (1) the pharmacokinetics of intranasal administration in humans, which are more variable than in rodent models; (2) the durability of neurotrophic effects with repeated dosing, since most studies use acute-administration designs; and (3) the extent to which the effects generalize outside the specific injury and recovery models where most clinical data have been collected. These are the gaps a careful reader of this literature should keep in mind.

The bottom line

Semax is one of the better-characterized nootropic peptides in the published literature. The neurotrophic mechanism — transient upregulation of BDNF, NGF, and their receptors — is well-supported across multiple laboratories. The human translational data are meaningful but concentrated in a specific clinical context.
